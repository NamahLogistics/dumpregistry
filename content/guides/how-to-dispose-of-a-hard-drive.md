# How to dispose of a hard drive (and wipe data first)

“Hard drive disposal” is really two jobs: **make the data unreadable**, then **recycle the hardware** as e-waste. Skipping the first step is how old tax returns and photo libraries leave the house still readable.

## Step 1: Decide how dead the data needs to be

Pick a level that matches your risk:

- **Factory / OS wipe** — fine for low-sensitivity home drives that still mount
- **Full-disk encrypt then wipe** — stronger when the drive still works
- **Remove and physically destroy** — platters drilled/shredded, or SSD physically destroyed, when policy or high sensitivity requires it
- **Certified destruction** — businesses and clinics use vendors with certificates of destruction

SSDs do not behave like spinning disks — a quick format is not enough. Prefer encrypt+wipe tools meant for SSDs, or physical destruction.

External USB drives and old DVR/NVR disks count too. Pull them before you recycle the chassis.

## Step 2: Recycle the drive as e-waste

Once data risk is handled:

- Municipal **e-waste** drop-off or events
- Retailer electronics take-back (policies vary for bare drives)
- Certified e-waste recyclers for bulk lots

Do not put bare drives in the blue bin or trash cart. See [hard drive](/materials/hard-drive) and [e-waste](/guides/how-to-dispose-of-ewaste).

## Whole computer vs bare drive

If the PC still runs, wiping the drive in place and recycling the [desktop](/materials/desktop-computer) or [laptop](/materials/laptop) is often simpler. If you need to keep the machine, remove only the drive and recycle that piece.

## What not to do

- Toss an unencrypted drive in a dumpster
- Microwave or “burn” a drive indoors
- Donate a PC with a live drive and no wipe
- Ship damaged lithium UPS batteries with the PC — separate [lithium](/guides/how-to-dispose-of-lithium-batteries) rules
