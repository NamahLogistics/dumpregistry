# How to dispose of a car battery safely

A lead-acid car battery is not household trash. It contains lead and corrosive acid, and nearly every U.S. city and state program treats it as banned-from-landfill material with a dedicated take-back channel. The good news: car batteries are one of the easiest hazardous items to recycle correctly because retailers want the cores.

## Why trash and recycling carts refuse them

Lead-acid batteries can:

- Leak acid that burns skin and damages truck equipment
- Contaminate landfill leachate with lead
- Short and spark if terminals contact metal in a load

Blue bins are wrong too. A battery in single-stream recycling is a contamination and safety incident, not “helpful recycling.”

## Best path for most people: retailer take-back

Auto parts stores and many service shops accept used car batteries year-round. Typical pattern:

- Bring the old battery when you buy a new one (core exchange)
- Or drop a used battery even without a purchase — call ahead to confirm
- Keep the battery upright in a tray or tub during transport

Core charges exist because the lead inside has value. Returning the old battery is how that system works. If a store refuses a cracked or leaking battery, escalate to your city or county household hazardous waste (HHW) facility.

## HHW and municipal options

When retail take-back is not available — damaged case, unusual battery size, or no store nearby — use:

- City or county HHW drop-off
- Periodic HHW collection events
- Transfer stations that explicitly list automotive batteries (not all do)

Bring proof of residency if your county requires it. Ask about quantity limits; some sites cap how many batteries a resident can bring per visit.

## Transport rules that keep you out of trouble

- Keep the battery **upright** and secure so it cannot tip in the trunk
- Cover terminals with tape or terminal caps to prevent shorts
- Do not stack tools or jumper cables bare across the posts
- Put a cracked battery in a plastic tub lined so acid cannot reach carpet
- Do not leave a battery in a hot car for days

If acid contacts skin or eyes, follow poison-control / SDS-style first aid and get medical help. Do not neutralize large spills with random household chemicals unless a professional instructs you.

## Lithium car and hybrid packs are different

This guide is about standard **12V lead-acid** car batteries. Hybrid and EV high-voltage packs are a different hazard class. Do not treat them like a Walmart core exchange item. Use dealer, manufacturer, or specialty programs listed for that vehicle. If you only have a small lithium jump-pack or power-station battery, see [lithium-ion battery disposal](/materials/lithium-battery) instead.

## Related batteries people confuse with car batteries

- [Household batteries](/materials/household-batteries) — AA/AAA and rechargeables; often HHW or retail drop boxes
- [Lithium-ion batteries](/materials/lithium-battery) — fire risk in trash trucks; never curb them loose
- Motorcycle and marine lead-acid batteries often follow the same retailer take-back pattern as car batteries — ask the store

## What not to do

- Toss a battery in a dumpster “just this once”
- Drain acid into a storm drain or onto soil
- Burn or dismantle a battery at home
- Pay a random hauler who cannot show where cores go
- Store dozens of old batteries in a garage for years — take them in batches
