# How to dispose of tires legally

Scrap tires are banned from ordinary household trash in most of the United States. They trap water (mosquitoes), burn dirty, and bounce around in landfill cells. Cities and states push residents toward **retailer take-back**, **transfer stations**, and **licensed scrap-tire handlers** — not the gray cart.

## The easiest path: buy new, leave old

When you replace tires, the shop usually takes the old ones for a per-tire recycling fee. That fee funds legitimate scrap-tire processing. Ask before the install:

- How many old tires are included
- Whether rims-on vs rims-off changes the price
- Whether they accept tires you bring without a purchase (sometimes yes, sometimes no)

## Transfer stations and municipal drop-off

If you have leftovers from a yard pile or a trailer:

- Call the transfer station for **per-tire pricing** and passenger vs truck tire rules
- Stack tires so staff can count them
- Expect higher fees for oversized agricultural tires

Some cities run periodic tire amnesty days. Those are capacity-limited — arrive early with a load that matches the published cap.

## Rims on or off?

- **Tire on rim** — often priced as a tire; some scrap yards want them separated
- **Bare rim** — usually scrap metal, not a scrap-tire fee — see [tire rims](/materials/tire-rims)

Do not burn tires off rims. Cut sidewalls only if a program instructs that for drainage — random curbside cutting still looks like dumping.

## What about “free tire pickup” ads?

Be careful. Legitimate haulers can show a business license and a tipping destination. Ultra-cheap curb pickup sometimes ends as roadside dumping — and investigators follow the last owner when possible. Prefer shops and municipal sites.

## What not to do

- Stack tires behind a shed for years (mosquito habitat + fire load)
- Dump tires in woods, vacant lots, or dumpsters you do not own
- Put tires in the blue recycling cart
- Assume bulk furniture day includes tires — it usually does not
